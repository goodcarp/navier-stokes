                                                   Abstract. For axisymmetric flows without swirl and compactly supported initial vorticity, we
                                                   prove the upper bound of t4/3 for the growth of the vorticity maximum, which was conjectured
                                                   by Childress [Phys. D, 2008] and supported by numerical computations from Childress–Gilbert–
                                                   Valiant [J. Fluid Mech. 2016]. The key is to estimate the velocity maximum by the kinetic energy
                                                   together with conserved quantities involving the vorticity.

1.3. Optimal vorticity growth and eroding dipole model by Childress. It is well known
to experimental physicists that vortex stretching occurs when two anti-parallel vortex rings move
towards each other. This so-called “head on collision” scenario has been investigated intensively
([29, 33, 37, 41, 14]), where it was observed that the maximal vortex strength increases together
with the Reynolds number. A simple but illuminating mathematical model of this scenario can be
given based on (1.4) (formally taking the Reynolds number to be infinite), by taking the initial
data to be of the form
(1.6)                                    ω0θ (r, z) = −φ(r, z) + φ(r, −z),
where φ ≥ 0 is a bump function supported near some point in (r, z), say (1, 1). This models two
anti-parallel vortex rings, and the key features are that the vorticity is odd symmetric in z and
non-positive for z ≥ 0. Under these two assumptions, it can be proved that ([10, 21])

numerical experiments which supports that the growth rate of t4/3 can be indeed achieved. They
also report that the optimal growth is attained by rescaled Sadovskii vortex patch–see [13, 23] for
more discussion related to this point.

support remains constant, it means that most of the volume filaments behind (this is what we have
attempted to depict in Figure 1). Hence the terminology “eroding” dipole in [6, 7] is appropriate.

1.6. Organization of the paper. The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In §2, we first
collect notations and recall estimates for the axisymmetric Biot–Savart kernel. The main results are
proved in §3. In §3.1, we prove the key R1/4 estimate on the velocity, which allows us to conclude

the appendix, we include the proof of the global velocity estimate (1.11), which could be of its own

                                               2. Preliminaries
2.1. Notations. We collect the notations used throughout the paper.
• It is convenient to introduce the half-plane Π := {(r, z) : r ≥ 0, z ∈ R}, as ω θ , ur , uz are functions
   defined on Π.
• Let us write ω instead of ω θ from now on.
• We use the letter R to denote the maximal support radius of ω, namely R is the smallest
   non-negative number satisfying ω(r, z) = 0 whenever r > R. We write R(t) when ω is time
   dependent.
• We write Dr,z (r, z) =D(r, z, r̄, z̄) := ((r − r)2 + (z − z)2 )1/2 as the distance between the points
   (r, z) and (r̄, z̄) in Π.
• For any k > 0 and any point (r, z) ∈ Π, we denote the disc centered at the point (r, z) with
   radius k as
                             Bk (r, z) := {(r, z) ∈ Π : D(r, z, r, z) < k}.
• We denote χ ∈ Cc∞ ([0, ∞); [0, 1]) as a non-increasing function that satisfies χ ≡ 1 on [0, 1/2] and
  χ ≡ 0 on (1, ∞).
• For non-negative expressions A and B, we write A ≲ B if there is an absolute constant C > 0
  such that A ≤ CB. We write A ∼ B if A ≲ B and B ≲ A.
